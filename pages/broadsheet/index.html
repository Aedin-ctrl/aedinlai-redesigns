A note on what I build, and why

I build the measuring end of things — instrumentation that has to be right, and the software that makes sense of what it reads. The appeal is not the cleverness available at that end of a system; it is that the work is checkable. Either the reading is the reading that happened or it is not.

A number that is quietly wrong is worse than no number at all. Everything downstream inherits it, every chart drawn from it is confident, and nobody can tell by looking. Most of the job is making sure that cannot happen, and almost none of that work is visible afterwards, which is the trade.

Where that has gone

In 2025 I spent a season on data centre infrastructure at SingleStore, where a private cloud platform went from 160 virtual machines to over 300. The number is not the interesting part. The interesting part was the tooling written along the way, which is what made the growth survivable rather than merely possible.

The same year, research hardware built with the Yale School of Medicine and the PEACE Foundation for a neurology study was co-authored and published in JAMA. The requirement there was not sophistication. It was that a reading taken in a clinic, by somebody with other things to worry about, be the reading that actually happened.

Both lights at once

Away from the bench I fence épée, which is the one weapon where both lights can come on at the same time. There is no right of way to argue about: if you both land inside forty milliseconds, you both landed, and the box says so.

Four years of that at national level — USA Fencing A25, All-American, #103 national and #122 global — and this season as captain at Northeastern, taking the team into the national championship. Captaincy is mostly logistics and occasionally fencing.
